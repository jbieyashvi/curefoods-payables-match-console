Foundations

Every value below is a CSS custom property on :root. Nothing in the interface hard-codes a colour or a size.

1.1 Colour

The product is white. The header, the rail, every card and every case screen are #FFFFFF; the greys below appear only where two surfaces genuinely need separating — the dashboard ground under its white cards, a table header, a card footer, a row hover, the canvas behind a scanned document. There is no cream, no beige and no warm grey anywhere. Green is the product colour: it marks what is matched, approved or normalised, and it carries the selected tab, the selected filter and every primary action. Red marks what is blocked, what failed to process, a commercial mismatch and every destructive action. Amber marks a difference worth reviewing that does not necessarily block, and appears as a glyph, a word, a badge or a 3px left rule — never as a large fill. Grey carries pending, skipped and secondary information. Blue carries informational rule outcomes, extracted invoice values and work in progress. Colour never carries meaning on its own: every state also has an icon and a word.

80% neutral surfaces and ink 15% status 5% green

The intended visual balance: eighty per cent white surfaces and ink, fifteen per cent functional status colour, five per cent green — the product mark and the outlines that show what is selected. A screen that reads as green or amber overall has broken it, and a filled yellow or peach panel anywhere is a defect.

Contrast. Body ink on white is 14.8:1 and secondary ink is 5.0:1. Every text tone clears 4.5:1 on the fill it sits on — --green-deep 7.3:1 on --green-bg and 8.2:1 on white, --red-deep 7.6:1, --blue 5.4:1, --slate 5.1:1, --amber-deep 4.9:1. White on the solid --green fill — every primary button and the product mark — is 6.3:1. A selected tab or filter segment keeps a white background and states its selection with --green text and a green underline, which reads at 8.2:1. One tone is graphical only and never carries readable text: --ink-3, the disabled grey — placeholders, dimmed glyphs and controls a person cannot use.

1.3 Spacing and layout

An 8px system with a 4px half-step for dense table interiors. Layout uses flex and grid with gap, never stacked margins.

One scroll, everywhere. The shell is a full-height flex layout: .frame is 100vh with overflow:hidden, the rail and the main column both run the full height with min-height:0, and exactly one element scrolls vertically — the content area under the case header. Because the case header and the tab bar are siblings of that scroller rather than children, they stay pinned without a single position:sticky rule. Wide comparison tables scroll horizontally inside their own card and never widen the page; every pane sets min-width:0 so a long identifier cannot force a page-level horizontal scrollbar. The one deliberate second scroller is the extracted-details pane on the invoice screen, which scrolls against the document viewer beside it — and below 1080px even that collapses back into normal page scrolling.

The dashboard is the one exception, by design. There the content column stops scrolling and the table card takes the remaining height instead: the column header stays pinned, the rows scroll inside the card, the horizontal scrollbar sits at the foot of the rows, and the pagination footer below it never leaves the screen — so the page you are on, the rows-per-page control and the next-page button are reachable without scrolling back up. The summary cards and the filter row hold their place above it, and the band between them is kept deliberately thin so the rows get the height: at 1440×900 ten invoice rows sit above the footer, and at 1280×800 seven.

And it is the one screen with a ground of its own. The dashboard content sits on --dash-ground #F7F8FA, a very light neutral grey — no cream, no beige, nothing warm — while the header, the rail, the five summary cards, the filter controls, the table and its pagination footer all stay #FFFFFF on a --line #E4E7EC border. The grey is what makes a white card read as a card. A pressed summary card therefore keeps its white face and states itself by lifting — a stronger border and a shadow — because filling it would sink it back into the ground. Case screens keep the plain white ground.

2.2 Tabs

Two mechanisms, used for different things. Case tabs switch the data layer you are looking at for one invoice pair; the selected tab is marked with a green rule, green label and semibold weight. Segmented filters narrow a list in place, always show a count, and always read as one control rather than a row of separate buttons.

Dashboard status

One rectangular bar rather than five loose controls: the tray is --tray #F8F9FA, the tabs are square-cornered and 26px tall, and the selected tab lifts out of the tray as a white card with a dark green label and a 2px green underline. Secondary filter — line items

A zero-count segment stays visible and disabled rather than disappearing, so the set of possible outcomes is always legible. Rule filters default to Blocked when the invoice is blocked, and to All otherwise.

The secondary filter is one object, not a row of buttons: a single --tray-2 #F6F8F7 tray with one --line border and an 8px radius, 3px of padding, 28px tabs and no border of its own on any tab — 36px overall, deliberately smaller than the 38px case tabs above it. A selected tab is a white face with a --green label at 600 and a 2px green underline; there is no green ring, so the 6px status dot beside the label stays the loudest thing in the tab. Labels sit at 12.5px and counts at 11.5px. Only a segment that names a single outcome carries a dot — All rules, All lines, Unmatched and Non-commercial are mixed groups and carry none.

The evaluation metadata stays hard right on the control's line and is the part that gives way: below about 1300px it drops to a line of its own rather than squeezing the tabs, and narrower still the tray itself scrolls sideways. The tabs stay one strip at every width — a tab never breaks onto a line of its own.

2.4 Filters and inputs

The dashboard puts every filter in one line above the table — search, brand, supplier, financial year — with the status segments, Excel export and the column chooser on the row above it. Nothing is hidden behind a “more filters” drawer, because an accountant changes two or three of these on every pass. Nothing carries a label beside it either: each control says what it filters from the inside, so the row reads as four fields rather than eight fragments.

The filter row26px · 11.5px type · one radius, one border, 8px gaps

No Brand, Supplier or Dates text beside the controls. The meaning lives inside each one — “All brands”, “All suppliers”, a calendar glyph and “Financial year 2026–27” — and the accessible name is still carried by a visually hidden <label>, so the field is announced as “Filter by brand” rather than as an unnamed combo box.

Widths at 1440px: search 472px (about 41% of the row), brand 184px, supplier 244px, financial year 224px, 8px between each — a third tighter than the 12px base gap, because this band is paid for in table rows.

Seven controls, one height. The four filters and the Run all, Export and Columns buttons on the status row above them all render at --h-dash 26px on the --r-dash 6px radius, with the same --line-strong border, the same white face, 11.5px type on a 14px line, 9px of inner padding, no vertical padding at all and 13px glyphs — so the whole band reads as one line of controls instead of two heights arguing with each other. The height is not chosen in the abstract: it is the height of the Testing only badge already sitting in that top bar, so the badge and the controls beside it share a baseline. Two controls keep a wider gutter on one side: a select for its chevron at 24px, the search for its 13px magnifier at 26px. Below 1360px the search takes a line of its own and the three filters sit underneath it at full size — two tidy rows, never a row of bare chevrons.

The row is a row of controls, not a panel: there is no container around it to pad, and the vertical rhythm is tight on purpose — 8px down from the status segments, 8px down to the table. Every pixel spent on the band above the table is a row of invoices lost from the bottom of it.

Date presets: Last 7 days · This month · Previous month · Financial year · Custom range. The chosen preset becomes the button label so the active window is readable without opening the menu, and the popover hangs from the right edge of its control so it never runs past the content.

2.8 Empty states

Four different emptinesses, and the interface never confuses them: nothing exists yet, nothing matched your filters, something is unavailable for a reason, or something failed. Each one says which, and each one offers the way out.

No invoice pairs yet

Failure is its own state. A failed export keeps the table in place and shows a red toast with a retry, rather than emptying the screen — the accountant’s filters and scroll position survive the failure.

Rules
Always reachableThe toggle sits inside the rail header in both states and is never removed by a display:none or clipped by an overflow:hidden. At 60px the rail cannot carry the 28px mark and the 30px toggle side by side without breaking the 60px header line it shares with the top bar, so the mark stands down and the toggle keeps the header.

Information hierarchy, screen by screen

Left: the order the screen is read in, top to bottom. Right: what that order is protecting.
1Dashboard — invoice pairs /

Reading order

  1. Page title and Run all. Run all is a temporary UAT control and is labelled as such.
  2. Five summary cards — Total pairs, Pending, Matched, Blocked, Skipped. Each shows an invoice count, a share of the total and a rupee volume, so “186 blocked” is immediately also “₹ 1.4 Cr held up”. White cards on neutral borders, equal in height, each named by a 3px status-coloured left rule — a summary the eye passes over on the way to the table, never a wall of dashboard tiles.
  3. Two compact control rows — the segmented status bar with Export and Columns on its right, then the filter line: search, brand, supplier, financial year.
  4. The table, taking the rest of the height: frozen PO / GRN / status columns, a pinned header, rows that scroll inside the card, and a pagination footer that stays put beneath them.

What the order protects

  • The accountant’s first question is how much is stuck, not which row is first. Volume sits above the table for that reason.
  • Filters are one line, never a drawer: narrowing by brand and status is the normal way in, not an advanced feature.
  • Frozen identity columns mean a horizontal scan for a difference never loses which invoice you are looking at.
  • Pagination inside the table container keeps the page itself from scrolling, so the control row stays reachable and the footer never has to be chased down the page.
  • The cards select the same filter the status bar does, so a pressed card stays quiet — a neutral tint, not a green ring. The named, counted segment above the table is what states the selection.
—Shared shell — Match Result /case/<id>/<tab>

Reading order

  1. Case summary, not a generic header — “Match Result”, then PO-CHSPR-CFI26-06847 / GRN-CFI26-CHSPR-0006, then the status chip with Last run 9h ago · Attempt 5.
  2. Three facts pinned to the right of the header: GRN value, invoice value, difference.
  3. Re-run Match as a secondary action, behind a confirmation.
  4. Six tabs — Purchase order, Goods received, Invoice, Line items, Rule violations, Oracle push. Counts appear on Line items and Rule violations.

What the order protects

  • The header answers “which case, what state, how stale” before any tab is opened.
  • The difference is visible from every tab, so the accountant never has to go back to the dashboard to recall the number they are chasing.
  • Re-run is deliberately not primary — the primary act on a blocked case is understanding it, not re-running it.
  • Tabs map exactly to the pipeline’s data layers, so the mental model matches the system.
2Purchase order/po

Reading order

  1. PO overview — PO number and status, seller, order date, delivery location, then subtotal, tax and total.
  2. Ordered items — SKU and item, ordered and confirmed quantity, UOM, unit price, discount, tax, total.
  3. Deliveries against this PO — a plain table of every GRN, with an explicit Current GRN badge.

What the order protects

  • The PO answers “what did we agree to buy”. Commercial terms lead; provenance and plumbing follow.
  • A PO with several deliveries is the common blocked case, so the delivery list is a first-class section rather than a footnote — and the current one is named, not merely tinted.
  • The matrix is powerful but wide; collapsing it keeps the default view readable while leaving it one click away.
  • A right-hand PO totals rail keeps subtotal, tax and total in view while the line table scrolls.
3Goods received note/grn

Reading order

  1. GRN overview — GRN and PO numbers, GRN status and date, delivery location, received amount, invoice availability, seller and GSTIN.
  2. Received items — SKU and item, delivered / received / returned quantity, UOM, GRN and PO price, variance, tax, total.
  3. Attachments (n) — one section, readable file labels, empty slots hidden entirely.
  4. Technical details, collapsed — 14 fields.

What the order protects

  • The GRN answers “what actually arrived, and do we have paper for it”. Invoice availability is a headline field because it decides whether matching can even run.
  • Price variance is computed and shown in the line table — GRN price less PO price, times received quantity — so the accountant does not do the arithmetic.
  • Five numbered attachment slots became one counted section; a storage filename is never shown where a readable label exists.
  • A right-hand rail carries GRN totals and Against the purchase order, with a note that quantities on this screen are the ones the rules compare.
4Invoice/invoice

Reading order

  1. Split screen — document at 42%, extracted details at 58%, divider draggable and remembered.
  2. Document toolbar — zoom, fit to width, rotate, full screen, open in a new tab, download.
  3. Extracted details in five groups: Invoice summary, Vendor, Tax, Buyer, Extraction quality.
  4. Re-extract Invoice as a secondary action, behind a confirmation.

What the order protects

  • Verifying an extracted value means looking at the document and the field at the same time; the split is the whole point of the screen and is never collapsed by default.
  • Confidence is a percentage, and only low-confidence fields are highlighted. A trusted field is simply a field.
  • Extraction quality — overall confidence, flag reasons, page count — is one card, so “why does this look wrong” has a single place to look.
  • Empty values are muted and spelled out, so a gap in extraction never reads as a zero.
5Line items — the review screen/lines

Reading order

  1. Legend and filters — All, Matched, Unmatched, Non-commercial, with the colour key stated in words.
  2. One block per item, with two labelled rows: Recorded in the PO or GRN, and Extracted from invoice.
  3. Columns in decision order — item and SKU, quantity and UOM, unit rate, discount, tax, subtotal, total. SKU and the source label stay frozen while the row scrolls.
  4. Normalisation, only where it happened — 420 nos × 2 kg = 840 kg, ₹ 760 per 2 kg = ₹ 380 per kg.
  5. The outcome, in words — “₹ 60 higher”, “3 kg excess”.

What the order protects

  • This is where the decision is actually made, so the comparison is horizontal and paired: the two numbers being compared sit in the same column, one above the other.
  • A matched item must never look like an error just because its name or raw unit differs. Green means matched or safely normalised, and normalisation is explained rather than hidden.
  • Amber is a different source value that needs attention but does not block; red is a genuine commercial mismatch. The legend says exactly that, in words, on the screen.
  • Raw JSON and per-row JSON controls are removed for UAT. The normalisation sentence replaces them for every case a reviewer actually needs.
6Rule violations/rules

Reading order

  1. Filters — All, Blocked, Flag, Note, Skipped, Passed. Defaults to Blocked when the invoice is blocked.
  2. Left column: the rule list, each row expandable in place.
  3. Inside an expanded rule — plain-language name, severity, affected field or line item, then expected, actual, difference, allowed threshold, and the normalisation reasoning where it applies.
  4. Right column: what you can do next — Re-run pipeline, Upload invoice and re-run, Create debit note, Request GRN correction — above an activity trail for the pair.

What the order protects

  • The difference carries the strongest emphasis in the expanded rule — largest type, strongest weight. Expected and actual support it; the machine rule code is secondary and mono.
  • Understanding and acting sit side by side, so deciding the correction never means losing sight of the reason for it.
  • The debit-note action is never labelled “send to vendor” — it drafts for an internal warehouse or kitchen contact, and the dialog names that person.
  • Every action here previews or confirms before anything is sent or processed.
7Oracle push/oracle

Reading order

  1. Status banner with the primary action — eligible, missing fields, already pushed, rejected, or blocked.
  2. One confidence legend, then the six sections in order: Identification and amounts, Supplier and business unit, Payment, Tax and source, Kitchen details, Line items and accounting distribution.
  3. Approve and push and Reject, both confirmed, plus Re-assemble with helper text explaining that it rebuilds the Oracle payload from the current matched data.

What the order protects

  • Missing fields are counted in the banner and the count is a link that scrolls to the first one, which then carries a red border and an inline message.
  • Confidence chips appear only below the trusted level. A wall of 100% chips trains people to stop reading them.
  • A blocked invoice gets a focused empty state that explains why the push is unavailable and links back to the blocking rule, rather than a disabled form.
  • Push and reject are the only genuinely outward acts in the product, so both are confirmed and both summarise what is about to happen.

06

Standing rules

The checks a new screen has to pass before it belongs in this product.
Never colour alone

Every state carries a glyph and a word as well as a tone. Blocked rows also carry a left rule; the current GRN also carries a badge.

Business fields first

Identifiers, UUIDs, raw timestamps, match IDs and attempt metadata go under Technical details. The default view is the one an accountant needs.

Name the layer

Nothing is shown as a bare fact. It is Recorded in the PO, Recorded in GRN, Extracted from invoice or Normalized for comparison.

Explain the normalisation

Whenever a rule ran on a converted value, the conversion is written out in the same place as the result, in the accountant’s own arithmetic.

The difference is the headline

On a violation, the difference gets the strongest emphasis. Expected, actual and threshold support it. The rule code stays secondary.

No raw JSON

Not on line items, not on rules, not under Technical details. If a payload matters, a field for it does.

Confirm before consequence

Anything that sends, drafts, re-runs, rejects or pushes opens a dialog that previews exactly what will happen and to whom.

One format per value

₹ 1,86,240.00, 17 Jul 2026, 1:02 PM, Available / Not available — everywhere, without exception.

Keyboard and screen reader

Visible focus on everything focusable, real labels on every control, aria-sort on sortable headers, aria-expanded on disclosures, live regions for toasts.

Green means selected

Selection is always green, and always drawn as an edge rather than a fill: a soft-green fill with a green left rule on the sidebar, a green label and a 2.5px green underline on the active case tab, and a white face with a green label and a 2px green underline on the selected segment of a segmented filter. Never black, never yellow, and never a solid green block that hides the status dot beside the label.

Amber is an exception, not a state

Amber appears for a GST mismatch, a low OCR confidence, a non-blocking variance and “Needs attention”. A selection, a navigation item or a build marker never earns it.

A filter always keeps its meaning

The label sits inside the control — “All brands”, “All suppliers”, “Financial year 2026–27” — with the accessible name in a visually hidden label. At 1280px the row wraps to a second line rather than shrinking a control, and Export and Columns stay reachable. A filter may never collapse into a bare chevron.

Read-only looks read-only

Input-style fields belong to the pre-push review, where editing is genuinely possible. Once an invoice is posted to Oracle its values are a record: plain value rows on hairlines, no boxes.

Dense, not raw

The dashboard runs at 48px rows, a 36px header, 12.5px cell text and 22px status chips — for people who read hundreds of lines a day — and an 11px floor on any text they have to read. Controls inside the working area hold a 34px floor. The dashboard's own filter band is the one deliberate exception: it runs at 26px, matched to the Testing-only badge beside it, which clears the 24×24px WCAG 2.2 target minimum but sits below what we ask of a control anywhere else — traded knowingly, because every pixel above the table is a row of invoices lost from the bottom of it. A long supplier or location truncates with an ellipsis and keeps its full value in a tooltip rather than widening the column. Familiar to an Excel user; never an imitation of Excel.

One neutral product

Brand is a filter and a column, never a skin. No brand marketing surfaces anywhere in the console.

Presentation only

No new backend capability, no changed matching logic, no altered routes or permissions. Where the field reference is silent — Oracle push fields — the interface follows the deployed product.
